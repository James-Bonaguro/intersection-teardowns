Market research briefing Prices checked October 20, 2026

What paid audits and teardowns actually sell for

Real published prices from named sellers, not theory: what audits and teardowns cost at every level of the market, which sales model actually makes money, and whether a standalone teardown holds up as a product for small local businesses.

As the whole business

Qualified no

Every first-hand account with numbers in this research makes its money downstream of the audit, in implementation, a rebuild, or a retainer. No successful named example was found of a mid-priced standalone written report sold cold to local businesses, with nothing behind it.

As a paid front door

Qualified yes

Sellers publish video teardowns at $250 to $550 and diagnostics at $1,450 to $2,950 whose fee credits in full toward the implementation work. Sold that way, as a front door to real work, it is a sellable product. What the evidence cannot show is sales volume: no teardown seller's revenue or order counts could be verified.

The price ladder

Typical published ranges by segment, drawn from the named sellers in section 2. The axis is logarithmic because the spread runs from a $25 marketplace gig to a £25,000 independent audit.

Ranges are the typical published bands from the research summary. UK prices (£250 GA4 audit, £5,000 to £25,000 VCMO) and the sequenceDM range in Canadian dollars sit inside these bands and are listed with their own currencies in the tables below.

The dead zone

A mid-priced written report, roughly $750 to $1,500, sold cold to local businesses with no implementation path behind it, sits in the worst position in the market: expensive enough to need a sales conversation, cheap enough that the seller cannot afford that conversation, and competing with free audits everywhere. The research found no successful named example of that shape.

04

Three sales models, and which one wins

By offer volume, free wins. By practitioner-reported economics, paid and credited wins. Pure standalone is real but narrow, and it only works at the two extremes of the price ladder.

Model 1 · Most common offer

Free audit as lead magnet

Indigo · WealthReach · Convertica · Disruptive Advertising · Go With Arch · WebFX (for 1 in 10)

By far the most common offer on agency and advisor-marketing sites. Usually automated or light-touch: breadth without the "how."

  • PPC Hero's distinction: a sales-process audit "does not recommend the how, it simply states what needs to be done." A paid audit "goes into significant detail and recommends the how."
  • Stackmatix, bluntly: agencies that offer free audits "are typically running a sales process, not a genuine diagnostic."
  • Go With Arch gives the written audit away with a 45-minute walkthrough: "You keep the written audit whether or not you hire us."
Model 2 · Strongest economics

Paid diagnostic, fee credited

MarginFix · MyWebAudit · Wayfront cases · Agency Mavericks

The model practitioners who write about their numbers actually recommend. The fee qualifies the buyer and de-risks the bigger engagement.

  • MarginFix: 100% of the audit fee becomes a credit toward implementation booked within 30 days.
  • Wayfront: a $2,000 growth strategy audit converted 35% of buyers into services worth $20,000+. A $2,500 funnel audit led most buyers into engagements of $25,000+.
  • Agency Mavericks prices discovery at roughly 10 to 20% of the overall project price.
Model 3 · Real but narrow

Pure standalone

Pages.Report · SEO Roast · VCMO · sequenceDM

Exists only at the extremes: very cheap and fast, where volume and speed make the unit economics work, or very expensive and independent, bought by mid-market buyers who specifically want no implementer attached.

  • Cheap end: $49 to $299 video teardowns, no call, 48-hour delivery.
  • Expensive end: VCMO at £5,000 to £25,000 sells independence itself as the product.
  • The middle of this model is where the dead zone sits.
"The audit isn't the business. It's the door. Most clients who complete the audit move into engagements worth $25,000 or more."

Mike Gammarino, Blueprint Partners, on his $2,500 sales funnel audit, via Wayfront's productized consulting research.

The counterargument, from a successful agency

PPC Hero argues that unless you are one of the best in the world, a solo shop already covering its living expenses, or drowning in tire-kickers, you should not charge for audits during the sales process at all: most agencies do not, so charging makes you the outlier, and even offering the fee back as a credit stacks the deck against you. Charging does weed out free-consulting seekers, at the cost of also weeding out some good prospects.

A number to not rely on: MarketingEye, an agency blog showing no methodology, sample or source, claims free audits convert at around 10 to 15 percent and paid-audit buyers close at 30 percent or higher. These are the only head-to-head percentages found anywhere in this research, and they are unsourced vendor content. They are recorded here so the gap is visible, not as evidence.

What this means for pricing to advisors

A $1,450 to $2,950 standalone audit would consume roughly 15 to 59 percent of a small advisory firm's entire annual hard-dollar marketing budget, against competitors giving the audit away and monetising a $750 to $795/month package. A $250 to $550 teardown, or a diagnostic credited toward implementation, fits the observed budget shape far better. This is inference from the prices and budgets above, not a surveyed fact.

One supporting detail for what a teardown should cover: Kitces 2026 figures, via SmartAsset, put online advisor directory listings at $0.28 of acquisition cost per $1 of new revenue, the lowest of 26 tactics measured, and SEO at $0.45. Listings, reviews and SEO are where advisor marketing return actually is, which supports the content of a teardown aimed at advisors even though it says nothing about paying for one.

06

Willingness to pay: what the evidence actually shows

Small businesses pay for audits in exactly three situations visible in this research: cheap and instant, as a de-risked first step toward a bigger engagement they already intend to buy, or as a genuinely independent second opinion at agency prices.

First-hand seller accounts

  • Standalone can work, then stops being standalone. A bootstrapped founder launched a flat $1,500 productized audit for ecommerce analytics, got 6 clients within 30 days, and reached $8,000/month within 90 days, from the audit converting into recurring work; audit clients later became subscribers to his SaaS. Single anecdote, self-reported, no independent verification. dev.to account
  • Standalone at launch can also be zero. Teardown Shop's founder states plainly in his launch post: 0 customers, 0 revenue on launch day, at $49 / $199 / $499 pricing. A priced page is not evidence of sales. The same caution applies to AuditIndie, whose $49 audit is still on a waitlist. Launch post
  • Buyers shelve audits. Sun BPO's founder: "I have watched owners pay $1,800 for an audit, file the PDF, and change nothing. An audit nobody implements is just a PDF." His firm folds the audit into month 1 of a retainer instead of billing it separately. His buy rule: pay $500 to $1,500 for a human-reviewed audit on a small site only when you have a specific question and someone ready to act within 60 days, and skip it if you are hiring an agency anyway, because you would pay twice. Sun BPO
  • The discovery fee almost always leads somewhere. MyWebAudit's agency guide prices discovery at $1,500 to $2,500, applies the fee as a credit toward the website project, and reports that in five years, of dozens of paid discovery sessions, only one did not lead to more paid work. Single-agency self-report; treat as anecdotal but specific. MyWebAudit
  • Independence is the strongest standalone pitch. X8 Agency's framing: a free audit is incentivized to serve the seller's next invoice, and its typical finding is something urgent and fixable by them. A paid fixed-fee diagnostic can honestly find "not much." X8 Agency
  • Paid-for-thinking is a step, not a business. Alt Agency's counter-take: paid discovery at the commonly heard $1,500 to $3,000 is "a bandaid" if the execution behind it is still fully custom. Alt Agency

Survey backdrop

No survey was found that asks small businesses directly whether they would pay for an audit alone. That specific willingness-to-pay evidence does not appear to exist in published form. The nearest context: the Ahrefs provider survey, as cited in pricing guides, puts the most common SEO project band at $2,501 to $5,000, average retainers at $1,348/month for freelancers against $3,209/month for agencies, and the most common hourly band at $75 to $100. Clutch survey work found 52 percent of small businesses invest in SEO. None of these isolate audit-only spend.

A hybrid structure worth knowing

One practitioner proposal structure from a detailed audit-proposal write-up: Option A, audit only, for genuine DIY implementers; Option B, audit plus Phase 1 remediation, priced 40 to 60 percent above A, which "most clients take"; Option C, full remediation with the audit included free. The author's rule: the audit is never free; sell it at a deliberately low fixed price, not at zero. Medium, audit proposal guide

07

Open questions: what could not be verified

Stated plainly, because the gaps are part of the answer.

  • Sales volume for any named teardown product. Pages.Report, SEO Roast and MarginFix publish prices and testimonials, and MarginFix claims 74 audits delivered and $58,000 in median findings. All of that is vendor-stated and none of it independently verified. No revenue figures for any standalone teardown seller were found.
  • Head-to-head conversion data for free versus paid versus credited audits, from any independent source. The only percentages found are the unsourced MarketingEye claims flagged in section 4.
  • Any paid standalone audit aimed specifically at financial advisors. Targeted searches surfaced only the free Indigo and WealthReach audits and paid advisor retainers. The absence may reflect search coverage rather than non-existence, but the direction is consistent across every search run.
  • Upwork and Fiverr transaction volume for audit listings. Prices are public; completed-order counts per listing were not in the fetched pages, so proven supply does not prove proven demand per listing.
  • AchyutamWeb's exact audit price. The fetched page text gave a site-wide price range of $79 to $5,549 CAD without isolating the audit's own price, so no figure is quoted for it here.
